## Summary

The linear displacement-based finite element method for solids derived from the principle of virtual work, its properties, the nonlinear incremental equations with Newton iteration, and linear dynamics: mode superposition, explicit and implicit direct integration, Rayleigh damping, how many modes to use, and the subspace iteration for $K\phi = \lambda M\phi$.

## Key points

- **Lecture 4, principle of virtual work:** the exact solution satisfies equilibrium $\tau_{ij,j} + f_i^B = 0$ in $V$ and $\tau_{ij}n_j = f_i^{S_f}$ on $S_f$, compatibility $u_i = u_i^{S_u}$ on $S_u$, and the stress-strain law ("differential formulation"). For the bar, multiplying $EA\,u'' + f^B = 0$ by a virtual displacement $\bar u$ (zero on $S_u$) and integrating by parts gives internal virtual work = external virtual work (A'); in 3D $\int_V \bar{\boldsymbol\epsilon}^T\mathbf C\boldsymbol\epsilon\,dV = \int_V \bar{\mathbf u}^T\mathbf f^B dV + \int_{S_f}\bar{\mathbf u}^{S_f T}\mathbf f^{S_f}dS$ (B). The variational form needs only $u$ continuous where the differential form needs $u''$; the two are equivalent.
- **Lecture 5, FE formulation:** $\mathbf u^{(m)} = \mathbf H^{(m)}\hat{\mathbf u}$ (1), $\boldsymbol\epsilon^{(m)} = \mathbf B^{(m)}\hat{\mathbf u}$ (2), the same interpolation for virtual quantities (3)-(4); choosing unit virtual displacement vectors one at a time gives $\mathbf K\mathbf U = \mathbf R$ with $\mathbf K = \sum_m\int_{V^{(m)}}\mathbf B^{(m)T}\mathbf C^{(m)}\mathbf B^{(m)}dV^{(m)}$, $\mathbf R_B = \sum_m\int\mathbf H^{(m)T}\mathbf f^{B(m)}dV$, $\mathbf R_S$ from surface tractions.
- **Lecture 6:** diagonal terms of $\mathbf K$ must be positive; $\mathbf K$ is symmetric; $\mathbf K$ is singular if rigid-body motions are possible, so they must be removed by boundary conditions, after which $\mathbf K$ is positive definite and the strain energy positive.
- **Lecture 7:** $\mathbf K$ is symmetric because $\mathbf C$ is; dynamics adds the inertia body force, $\mathbf M\ddot{\mathbf U} + \mathbf K\mathbf U = \mathbf R$ with the consistent mass $\mathbf M^{(m)} = \int\rho^{(m)}\mathbf H^{(m)T}\mathbf H^{(m)}dV$ (5). Four-node 2D element: bilinear $h_i$, equal to 1 at node $i$ and 0 at the others, $\sum h_i = 1$; they give constant strain, rigid-body motion and compatibility. Element nodal forces $\mathbf F^{(m)} = \int\mathbf B^{(m)T}\boldsymbol\tau^{(m)}dV$ (A): they balance the applied loads at every node (property I) and each element is in equilibrium under its nodal forces (property II); differential equilibrium is not satisfied pointwise, and the error falls as the mesh is refined or the order raised.
- **Lecture 10, nonlinear analysis:** analysis types I linear (strain below about 0.04, constant $E$), II materially nonlinear only, III large displacements with small strains (e.g. shell buckling), IV large displacements and large strains, V contact (changing boundary conditions). Incremental equilibrium ${}^{t+\Delta t}\mathbf F = {}^{t+\Delta t}\mathbf R$ (1) solved by Newton-Raphson: ${}^{t+\Delta t}\mathbf K^{(i-1)}\Delta\mathbf U^{(i)} = {}^{t+\Delta t}\mathbf R - {}^{t+\Delta t}\mathbf F^{(i-1)}$ (A), $\mathbf U^{(i)} = \mathbf U^{(i-1)} + \Delta\mathbf U^{(i)}$ (B), started from the state at $t$ (C); stop when the displacement increments (e.g. $10^{-6}$, $10^{-8}$) or the out-of-balance load become small; too large steps fail to converge. Modified Newton keeps $\mathbf K$ from the start: more iterations, cheaper each, but $\mathbf F^{(i-1)}$ must be computed correctly every iteration.
- **Lecture 15:** $\mathbf M\ddot{\mathbf U} + \mathbf C\dot{\mathbf U} + \mathbf K\mathbf U = \mathbf R(t)$ (1) is solved by mode superposition or direct integration. Free vibration $\mathbf U = \boldsymbol\phi\sin\omega(t - t_0)$ gives the generalised eigenproblem $\mathbf K\boldsymbol\phi = \omega^2\mathbf M\boldsymbol\phi$, $\det(\mathbf K - \lambda\mathbf M) = 0$; eigenvectors are normalised $\boldsymbol\phi_i^T\mathbf M\boldsymbol\phi_i = 1$ and are M-orthonormal, $\boldsymbol\phi_i^T\mathbf M\boldsymbol\phi_j = \delta_{ij}$, $\boldsymbol\phi_i^T\mathbf K\boldsymbol\phi_j = \omega_i^2\delta_{ij}$; $\omega = 0$ for a rigid-body mode (no strain energy). With $\mathbf P = \boldsymbol\Phi$ and no damping the equations decouple: $\ddot x_i + \omega_i^2 x_i = \boldsymbol\phi_i^T\mathbf R$.
- **Lecture 16:** Rayleigh quotient $\rho(\mathbf v) = \mathbf v^T\mathbf K\mathbf v/\mathbf v^T\mathbf M\mathbf v$, bounded by $\omega_1^2$ and $\omega_n^2$; modal equation with damping ratio $\ddot x_i + 2\omega_i\xi_i\dot x_i + \omega_i^2 x_i = r_i$ (5); modal initial conditions ${}^0\mathbf X = \boldsymbol\Phi^T\mathbf M\,{}^0\mathbf U$ (7); a body released in one mode vibrates only in that mode. Central difference (explicit): with $\mathbf C = 0$ and diagonal $\mathbf M$ no factorisation is needed; stable only for $\Delta t \le T_n/\pi$, $T_n = 2\pi/\omega_n$ the smallest period; consistent mass $\int\rho\mathbf H^T\mathbf H\,dV$, lumped mass by placing a quarter of the mass at each node of the 4-node element. Implicit methods (trapezoidal rule, a special case of Newmark) use equilibrium at $t + \Delta t$.
- **Lecture 18:** mode superposition with diagonal $\boldsymbol\Phi^T\mathbf C\boldsymbol\Phi = \mathrm{diag}(2\omega_i\xi_i)$ (3); central difference stability $\Delta t \le \Delta t_{cr} = T_n/\pi$ (8); trapezoidal rule (constant average acceleration) gives $(c_1\mathbf M + c_2\mathbf C + \mathbf K)\,{}^{t+\Delta t}\mathbf U = {}^{t+\Delta t}\hat{\mathbf R}$ (12) and is unconditionally stable; stability comes first, accuracy still needs a small enough step. Rayleigh damping $\mathbf C = \alpha\mathbf M + \beta\mathbf K$: $\alpha + \beta\omega_i^2 = 2\omega_i\xi_i$ (14) fitted at two frequencies (15), then $\xi_i = \alpha/(2\omega_i) + \beta\omega_i/2$ (16), mass-proportional at low and stiffness-proportional at high frequency.
- **Lecture 19:** how many modes: initial conditions or load distributions that use only some modes excite only those; for a harmonic load $\ddot x_i + 2\omega_i\xi_i\dot x_i + \omega_i^2 x_i = \sin\hat\omega t$ the dynamic load factor (dynamic over static peak) shows no response for very large $\hat\omega$ and a static response for very small $\hat\omega$; choose a cut-off frequency above the highest load frequency (Fourier analysis of the loads) and a mesh that represents the response up to it; error measure $\epsilon_p(t)$ from the residual of the truncated solution; static correction $\mathbf K\Delta\mathbf U_S = \Delta\mathbf R$ for the load part not carried by the retained modes. Mode superposition suits low-frequency excitation (earthquake, road), not wave propagation.
- **Lecture 23, $K\phi = \lambda M\phi$:** M- and K-orthogonality is not sufficient for eigenvectors unless $q = n$; Rayleigh-Ritz with Ritz vectors $\boldsymbol\Psi$: $\tilde{\mathbf K}\mathbf x = \rho\tilde{\mathbf M}\mathbf x$, $\tilde{\mathbf K} = \boldsymbol\Psi^T\mathbf K\boldsymbol\Psi$ (4)-(5), accuracy not known in general; subspace iteration: $\mathbf K\bar{\mathbf X}_{k+1} = \mathbf M\mathbf X_k$ (inverse iteration on $q$ vectors) followed by a Rayleigh-Ritz step (b)-(e); convergence rate $\lambda_i/\lambda_{q+1}$; use $q$ vectors for $p$ wanted eigenvalues, $q = \max(2p, p + 8)$ called "quite conservative", the textbook's $q = \min(2p, p + 8)$ with a Sturm sequence check.
