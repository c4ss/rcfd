# Documentation process review, adapted for an agent swarm

A review of how rcfd records plans, decisions, evidence and status, measured against a framework drawn from published authors and then adapted for a project built by many AI agents under one maintainer. It gives a score per clause as the repository was read, twelve proposed improvements, and a projected score if they are adopted. It is model output (Claude, 2026-11-18, repository at commit 205889bf) and has not been reviewed by a human. On 2026-11-18 the maintainer said to put it in place; what exists now and what does not is under "What has been put in place" at the end. The maintainer answered its seven questions on 2026-11-18; the answers are at the end, and the improvements were revised to follow them. The scores describe the repository as read, before the answers.

How it was made. The review for a human team was attacked by a red-team agent, each objection was checked against the repository by a separate validator agent, and the text was rewritten; this was done four times (46 objections confirmed, 18 partly, none refuted). The adaptation for a swarm was then added and put through the same loop three times (32 objections confirmed, 9 partly, none refuted; 18, 16 and 10 standing after validation, none of high severity in the last two). This text applies the third wave and has not itself been attacked. Author, red-teamer and validator are the same model, so by this review's own premise P3 the loop is not an independent check. The published authors did not write about agent swarms: the adaptation is this review's reasoning from their premises, tested against six days of this project's own swarm operation. On 2026-11-18 ten ideas from a second review, supplied by the maintainer, were attacked and checked in the same way (57 criticisms: 48 accepted, 9 in part, none refused). Seven entered in a changed form, in I1 to I6; three did not and are under "Not proposed".

The agents' house rules it cites are outside the repository, in the maintainer's work folder: `w49i-work/AGENT_RULES.md` and its predecessor `w49i-work/briefs/common.md`.

## Scope and limits

S1. Subject: how the project records plans, decisions, evidence and status, and how readers (people and agents) get at them. Not the quality of the solver.

S2. Two processes exist, each with written rules. Campaign stream: registration ADRs under AGENT_RULES.md, 2026-11-12 onward (509 ADRs, 421 registrations). Studio stream: items F-800 to F-838 and F-848 (40; counts of 39 below are of F-800 to F-838) under docs/studio-guardrails.md, ADR 0800 and ADR 0801. Each score names its stream.

S3. Time base: the project is 36 days old (2026-10-13 to 2026-11-17), the campaign six days, the Studio stream two days. "The campaign period" below means commit 49fbf1a3 (2026-11-12) to HEAD: 3,122 commits, 500 merges.

S4. Comparison class: one maintainer directing a lead agent and many implementation agents in parallel worktrees on a clean-room numerical code, who needs to trust the result without reading it all. The project already works this way (G1, G4, G9).

S5. Method limits: git cannot show code written before a registration but committed after it; author dates can be set; no network, so hosted CI results are known only from what the repo records; counts of word patterns depend on the pattern and are given with the pattern. Two traces outside the repository were used for G9 only: the push log of origin/main and file times in the lead's build folder. They show the last build of each test binary, not whether it passed.

S6. Not examined: SECURITY.md, docs/releasing.md, DESIGN.md, docs/architecture.md, docs/audit/, docs/investigations/, docs/designs/; whether *_known_miss test bodies changed after their results (604 functions; none was removed except with deleted crates); whether any "from memory" citation is wrong; whether the lead is a separate session from each author; what the maintainer accepted in conversation without a record; how the lead is run (its prompts, its tools, how it starts agents), which is not in the repository; the lead's load; cost in tokens, money and machine hours; whether the scanner for tests that cannot fail (docs/test-process.md section 6) is run on campaign items. ## What changes when the workers are agents

Six premises that hold for human teams do not hold for a swarm. Singer (P1) and Nygard (P2) are sources of the framework below; Reinertsen and Brooks are cited in addition; P3, P5 and P6 are this review's own. Each paragraph names the premise, what replaces it, the evidence from this project where there is some, and the clauses it adds or rewords.

P1. Scarcity moves. Singer's appetite and Reinertsen's queues limit work by the team's time, because people's time is what runs out. Agents produce code and text at little cost in time, so what runs out is the maintainer's attention, compute on the one shared machine, and whole-system verification (G26). A limit on work has to be counted in those units. The lead's serial work may be a further limit; it was not measured. Clauses 2b, 4c, 7d.

P2. The documents are the memory. A human team carries context in people's heads and in conversation; documents supplement it, which is why Nygard asks for short records. An agent starts each item knowing only what it is given and what it reads, and agents do not talk to each other. The record is the only channel between them, so what an agent was told (its brief) is part of the record, and the cost that matters is reading, not writing. A long record is acceptable as an audit trail if a generated index and a stated reading list sit on top of it. Clauses 3c, 3e, 6d.

P3. Trust comes from mechanism. Peer review works among people because reviewers judge independently and answer for their judgement. Agents of one model may share an error. This is reasoning: the project has no measurement of it. Its three same-kind second readings (G27) measured agreement or row accuracy, not detection of a known error, and where they differed the second reader was the stricter. An agent cannot be held to account. Trust therefore rests on criteria fixed before the work, records that are not rewritten, checks a machine runs, a checker whose errors are not the author's, and a record of what produced each result, so that a class of error can be traced to a rules version, a brief or a model. Clauses 4d, 8c.

P4. Coordination cost is contention. Brooks's cost of adding people is the conversations between them. Agents coordinate only through files and one hub, so the cost appears as conflicts on shared files: 96 of the 500 merges of the campaign period conflict when replayed, most often in the roadmap pair, docs/reference-data.md and generated files (G25). The answer is one writer for each hand-kept shared file (rule 9 does this for the roadmap in the campaign stream), generated files regenerated, never merged by hand (a branch that changes a generator's input regenerates on the branch where a test requires it; on a conflict the lead takes either side and reruns the generator on main before the push), and pages that many agents append to merged in a way that cannot conflict. Clause 5b.

P5. A written priority does not steer by itself. With people, a stated priority steers because the same few people must choose what to do next. With agents, any line of work that splits into items can absorb any number of them, so work goes where items can be issued, and a priority item that is blocked simply receives none. This is reasoning; the project's record fits it but has another reading too (G22: most new work followed the maintainer's later instructions, and the written priority was not restated). Clause 1b.

P6. The worker writes the next work. In a human team someone else usually sets the task and the bar. Here the agent proposes its follow-up items with their acceptance (rule 9) and sets its own numeric limits (rule 1): 435 of the 490 items added in the campaign period took their acceptance from an agent's addendum, 407 of them unchanged by the lead (G24). Criteria fixed first stop a bar from being moved; they do not stop it from being set low. Clause 4f.

Clean-room. The project's rule is about sources, not exposure: nothing is derived from vendor material and every algorithm and number has a cited public source (CONTRIBUTING.md; rule 4 adds "no vendor documentation" and "say when citing from memory"). For agents two things can be shown: what an agent could reach and fetched, and that someone checks each citation. Neither is recorded today (G18, G28). Clause 8d.

## Framework

No letter grades. Each clause has two fields: Holds (yes, partly, no) and Kept by (machine, convention, nothing). The table shows which clauses remain open. Clauses marked (swarm) were added or reworded for agents; the premise is named.

| # | Dimension | Sources and what is taken from each | Clauses |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Direction | Rumelt, Good Strategy Bad Strategy: diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent action. | a. A current priority is written in one place. b. (swarm, P5) New work is issued on the lines the current priority names. c. Each item names the target it serves. |
| 2 | Bounded bets | Singer, Shape Up: appetite and circuit breaker. | a. Each piece of work is bounded before it starts. b. (swarm, P1) A line of work has a budget, counted in registrations issued and in maintainer questions. |
| 3 | Record system | Nygard, Documenting Architecture Decisions: numbered records, not edited after acceptance. | a. Records are not rewritten. b. A record's kind is known. c. (swarm, P2) A record's current outcome can be read by a program. d. Records can be found by subject. e. (swarm, P2) What each agent was told is kept. |
| 4 | Evidence | Nosek et al., The preregistration revolution: criteria before data. Humble and Farley, Continuous Delivery: a known-good mainline. | 7 | Flow and feedback | Goldratt, The Goal: find the constraint and subordinate to it. Forsgren, Humble and Kim, Accelerate: short feedback. | a. The constraint is named. b. | 8 | Governance and provenance | Gawande, The Checklist Manifesto: written, versioned checklists. The project's clean-room rule (CONTRIBUTING.md). | a. Rules are in the repo and versioned. b. (swarm) Sources of algorithms and numbers are recorded and checked, and what an agent fetched is kept. |

I11. Diff-selected tests before the merge, within a budget (the maintainer's strategy, 2026-11-18; lead writes the selection; M). The directions: trim testing to the least validation that an agent cannot do for itself (Q3), never stop merging, and keep the gate from going red. The strategy: before a merge reaches main, the gate tests that its diff selects are run, up to 30 minutes; what does not fit in the budget is left to run, and if need be to fail, in CI on main. No test is switched off to make a run pass.

- Selection. Always: the item's registered tests, baseline_hashes, docs_site when docs/ is touched, and the lints of I1. Then the tests of the crates the diff touches, then the tests of the crates that depend on them (the crate graph is acyclic and a test keeps it so). Within each group, tests that failed in CI on main in recent runs come first, then the others by recorded run time, shortest first, so that the budget buys the most tests.
- Budget. Selection stops at 30 minutes of recorded run time, taken from the times file the full run already writes; a test with no recorded time counts at a default. The 30 minutes is a cap, not a target: over 194 agent logs the summed test time of an item is a median 17 seconds, and compiling dominates.
- Where it runs. In the push script of I2, on the merged tree, before the push. A merge whose selected tests fail is not pushed.
- What is left out is written down. The ledger line of the push names the number of selected tests that did not fit and their recorded minutes, so a later failure of one of them in CI on main is an expected kind of event, not a surprise.
- The tests left out start at once after the push, in the background, for that one merge. A failure is then tied to a single merge within the hour instead of being found in the next full run among a day's merges. It blocks nothing.
- CI on main runs everything, as now, but split into parallel jobs so that a full run finishes in about an hour and can run several times a day. A failure there is attributed to its ADR and the lead issues the fix before any new work on that line (I2). Main may be red on a left-out test until its fix lands. A tag `verified` moves to a commit when a full run on it is green, and the ledger shows merges since `verified`.
- Capacity is the thing to watch. At about 72 merges into main a day, a push that used its whole budget every time would need 36 machine-hours a day on the one shared machine, where rule 7 already forbids agents whole-crate suites. I1(m) therefore prints, per push, the minutes used and the tests left out. If a queue forms, the lead merges several branches and runs one selection over their joint diff; a failure is then traced within the batch by the bisect the attribution script already has.
- The full run is still trimmed (Q3): one issued item classifies what it runs into what the push already ran, what no single agent can run for itself (interactions between merges, the other operating system, runs too heavy for the budget), and tests that cannot fail (docs/test-process.md section 6).

A caution from this review's own findings stands: the agent measured by a limit sets it (4f), and whether an agent of the same kind catches another's error is unmeasured (P3). Before a test is dropped from the full run, "an agent can validate it itself" should mean that a failure would be visible to someone other than the author. Recurring: none by hand; the selection is in the script. Moves 7c; moves 4c to known.

## Refinements against the sources (2026-11-18)

The proposed process was read once more against the authors of the framework, clause by clause, for what it still left open. Five changes follow. They are the architect's and have not been attacked or checked by the loop.

- Each item names its line (Rumelt: coherent action; clause 1c). In the priority statement of I6 a line is a roadmap_progress.py category; every item is listed in a category (0 of 754 unlisted) and I4 makes the script fail on one that is not. An item's target is therefore the place of its category in the priority statement, and I1(f) prints done, partial and todo per line in the order of that statement. No new field.
- The first page leads with outcomes (Cagan: outcome over output; clauses 1b, 6a). docs/roadmap-overview.md opens with the state of the lines the priority statement names, in order, taken from I1(f). The share of all items done stays on the page, lower down, as a count of what has landed and not as the headline.
- I12. Size as a registered prediction (Singer: appetite; Reinertsen: count in the unit that is scarce; clauses 2b, 6d). Maintainer approves the rule; lead and agents apply; S. Time does not tell items apart: for 375 campaign registrations the median time from registration to merge is 27 minutes and 90 % merge within 95, while their items stand at 172 done and 202 partial. A size is therefore five values in units scarce for a swarm: the expected outcome of this registration (done or partial), further registrations expected, heavy runs on the shared machine, questions for the maintainer, and bytes to read. The brief carries the lead's size and the registration the agent's, fixed before any code as the limits are. The results addendum states the actuals, and I1(m) reports the calibration per category. Only the lead's size counts against a budget (I6); the agent's is scored and nothing depends on it, because the measured agent should not set what it is charged (P6). Nobody plans by either until some weeks of calibration exist. Recurring: five values in each brief (lead) and in each registration (agents).
- The process is measured (Deming: plan, do, check, act; Forsgren, Humble and Kim: a few measured keys). I1 gains a section (m), printed with the weekly list: merges into main since the newest green full run; registrations per line against its budget; unanswered questions and the age of the oldest; partial items without a gap; tests without a primary ADR; citations from memory remaining; and the size calibration of I12. The rows of the score table that rest on a count can then be scored again from the report instead of by a review like this one.
- The rules stay a checklist (Gawande: short, used at the moment of work). The rules file is 59 lines today and I3 adds four rules. It keeps a length limit that the maintainer sets; each rule is one short paragraph, its reasons live in the ADR that introduced it, and a new rule replaces or merges with an old one when the limit is reached.

## Closing the remaining gaps (2026-11-18)

Fifteen rows of the forecast read "partly". The changes below close most of them. Each is one rule, one check or one issued item. The maintainer decided three of them: no stop of merging (see I11), no standing daily read by another model, and yes to the validation rule. A second round of eight changes, chosen by the maintainer on 2026-11-18 from ten further proposals, is marked (round 2). None has been attacked by the loop.

- Append-only records (3a). Every ADR is append-only from its creating commit: I1(c) checks it for all ADRs, with today's exceptions in a file that only shrinks. No new addendum is added to an ADR over a length the maintainer sets; a further decision in that subject is a new ADR. This ends the growth of ADR 0801.
- Kind in the name (3b). A new ADR file name contains one of a fixed list of kind words; the 103 older names without one are exceptions. Checked by I1.
- Mutation as a patch, verified on every push (4d; round 2). Each registration commits one of its planned mutations as a patch file beside its test. The push script applies the patch, runs that one test and expects it to fail, then removes the patch. It adds one rebuild of the touched crate to each push.
- Mutations the author did not write (4d; round 2; approved by the maintainer). A mutation tool (cargo-mutants, a development tool and not a dependency of any crate) runs in the full run on the lines each merge changed and lists the mutants that no test caught. The list goes in the ledger with the item and its ADR. This is a check the author cannot weaken. It covers Rust only: the Studio's TypeScript has the committed patch and no generated mutants. Its run time on this workspace is not known and is measured by the item that adds it.
- Kind of evidence, with a rule that reads it (4e; approved by the maintainer). The checks table of a registration gains a column "kind" with four values: unchanged default output, code verification, solution verification, validation. An item whose acceptance claims agreement with a measured case is not done without a passing row of kind validation. I1 checks the column; the status rule applies the second sentence. Some items now done may return to partial when the rule is first applied.
- Margin of each limit (4f). The checks table gains a column "margin": the limit divided by the agent's own estimate, which rule 1 already asks for. I1 lists rows with a margin above 3. It reports; it does not refuse.
- A tolerance table (4f; round 2; the maintainer approves it once). One page in docs/process/ gives the allowed limit for each kind of check, for example observed order of accuracy, grid convergence, agreement with a published value, bit-identity. A limit in a registration cites its source: the table, a published reference with its place in it, or the acceptance of the parent item. The bar is then set by class of check by the maintainer, not by the agent it measures.
- Self-sourced limits are held (4f; round 2). I1 lists every limit whose only source is the agent's own estimate, and the lead does not issue the item's code until the limit has a source from the list above. The registration is still committed first, so this is a check on the registration, before any code.
- Acceptance that can fail (4f). The lead enters a follow-up item only if its acceptance names a number or a named reference, or hands its limits to the registration in the usual sentence. Checked by I1(a) on docs/roadmap.json.
- One-line note entries (5a). From a date the maintainer sets, an entry in an item's note is one line: ADR number, date, outcome, one sentence. The history stays in the ADR. Checked by I1(a) on entry length; older entries are left as they are.
- A dated first page (6a). The hand-written part of docs/roadmap-overview.md carries a "reviewed on" date. I1 flags the page when that date is older than the current priority statement.
- Every area listed (6c). The list of category and page pairs of I1(i) covers all 15 categories; a category with no page is printed as "no page". The "what was built" section of each area page is generated (round 2): a script writes the list of the area's done items from the one-line note entries, under a hand-written introduction, so the section is current by construction and checked like the other generated files.
- A limit on reading (6d). After two weeks of the size data of I12, the limit on bytes to read for one item is the 90th percentile observed; the lead splits a brief that exceeds it or points it at an area's reading list.
- An audit of the lead (8b). One issued item a week: a sample drawn by a registered seed of the lead's status decisions, each checked against the decision rule its registration fixed, and of its briefs against the rules. Findings of scope go on the maintainer's weekly list.
- Citations do not grow and are sampled (8d). The count of citations from memory in I1(e) may not rise. One issued item a week re-reads five citations, drawn by seed, against their sources. Every citation carries a locator (round 2): an equation, table or page number, or a DOI; I1(e) lists those without.

## Questions and the maintainer's answers (2026-11-18)

Q1. Should the two rule sets (campaign and Studio) become one? Answer: yes. Effect: I3 writes one set of process rules for both streams; 8a and 4b (Studio) then follow the campaign's rules.

Q2. Why was the roadmap generator and milestones file of 2026-10-31 not merged, and should it be? Answer: because of orchestration workflow issues. Asked afterwards whether to revive it: yes. Effect: I4 issues the item that brings it onto main.

Q3. After a first green run on current main, how many merges may main run ahead of it? Answer: as many as one gate a day can handle, refined over time; and testing is to be trimmed to the least validation that Claude cannot do for itself. Effect: I2 takes one day's merges as the limit; I11 is added.

Q4. What is maintainer acceptance, where is it written, and what will the maintainer review, at what rate? Answer: it is the green light for work to be scoped or prioritised, not for detail work. Effect: acceptance is given per line of work in the priority statement (I3, I6), not per result. "Done" is the lead's decision by the registered rule, the wording "pending the maintainer's acceptance" goes (I4), and the queue of 7b holds questions of scope and priority only. No person checks results by design, which puts more weight on machine checks and on I9.

Q5. Who checks "from memory" citations against sources? Answer: they are to be removed as far as possible. Effect: I10 is added.

Q6. Is a model other than the authors' available for the trial of I9, and which model wrote the sampled items? Answer: Claude has been used for almost everything, and any model is welcome to work on the project. Effect: I9 can run with a reader of another model. Which Claude model wrote an item is still not recorded (G23); I8 records it from now on.

Q7. Agents read outside their worktree and some have used the network. Should either be restricted, and should what they fetch be kept? Answer: no. Effect: agents may fetch public sources, which I10 relies on; what they fetch is not kept, so a citation is checked by reading the cited source again, not from a record of the fetch.

Two further decisions of the same day. What the lead does when a day's gate is not green: failing tests are linked to the record they fail against (ADR or experiment), which is why that link must exist; I2 carries it. Whether commits carry an agent marker: no (I7).
